# The Invisible Spark That Invented Radio

## Script
In 1887, a German physicist named Heinrich Hertz darkened his laboratory and set up two shiny brass spheres.
Between them sat a tiny air gap wired to a high-voltage induction coil ready to crackle with lightning.
Across the room—completely disconnected with zero wires touching—sat a simple copper ring with its own microscopic gap.
Hertz flipped the switch, and bright blue sparks snapped loudly between the primary brass balls.
He leaned over the dark wooden table and squinted at the detached copper loop standing meters away.
Out of nowhere, a tiny twin blue spark jumped cleanly across the empty gap inside the disconnected ring.
No copper cable connected them, yet pure electrical energy had leaped invisibly straight through empty air.
Hertz held up metal sheets and wooden doors between the coils to test what could pass through.
Solid wood let the phantom spark cross cleanly, while metal bounced the invisible waves right back like a mirror.
He had just captured electromagnetic radio waves—the exact invisible ripples James Clerk Maxwell predicted with pure math.
Think of the sparking transmitter like tossing a pebble into a still pond to send ripples outward.
When those invisible ripples reach a second tuned loop across the water, they shake electrons back and forth until a spark leaps.
When reporters asked what use his invisible sparks could possibly have, Hertz shrugged and called it pure curiosity.
Yet within fifteen years, Marconi stretched those same sparks across the Atlantic Ocean, launching radio, Wi-Fi, and satellites.
Every wireless message buzzing into your phone right now started as a microscopic blue spark in a dark room.
