# He Exploded a Wooden Barrel With One Cup of Water

## Script
In 1646, a twenty-three-year-old mathematician named Blaise Pascal made an impossible bet in front of a skeptical crowd.
He claimed a single cup of water could blow apart a massive oak barrel reinforced with thick iron hoops.
Everyone laughed because the barrel held hundreds of liters and looked strong enough to survive a cannonball.
Pascal filled the barrel completely to the brim, sealed the wooden lid tight, and inserted a razor-thin ten-meter vertical tube.
Then he climbed a wooden ladder high above the cobblestone courtyard holding nothing but one small brass pitcher.
The crowd below squinted up, expecting the tiny trickle of water to do absolutely nothing at all.
Pascal tipped the pitcher and poured one single cup of water slowly down the tall narrow pipe.
As the thin column climbed ten meters high, pressure exploded outward across every square inch inside the barrel.
Suddenly, wooden staves groaned, iron bands snapped like dry twigs, and water erupted across the street.
The astonished crowd scrambled backward soaking wet while Pascal climbed down without spilling a drop.
You see, fluid pressure depends only on vertical depth, not on the total weight or width of the water.
A hair-thin column ten meters tall pushes just as hard at the bottom as a ten-meter-deep swimming pool.
Every millimeter of barrel wall felt that towering water height pressing outward all at once.
Today, hydraulic car lifts use Pascal's exact principle so a tiny pump can hoist an entire truck into the air.
So never underestimate a skinny column of liquid—give it enough height, and one cup can shatter oak and iron.

## Facts
- Blaise Pascal's 1646 hydrostatic barrel demonstration (Pascal's barrel, Wikipedia).
- Hydrostatic pressure `P = rho * g * h` depends strictly on fluid column height `h`, independent of vessel volume (physics calculation).
- Hydraulic multiplication (`F2 / A2 = F1 / A1`) powers modern hydraulic car lifts and brakes (Wikipedia).
