# Why Air Speed Makes Heavy Airplanes Fly

## Script
In 1738, a Swiss mathematician named Daniel Bernoulli discovered a bizarre rule about moving air and water.
He noticed that whenever a fluid speeds up, the pressure it pushes sideways drops dramatically.
Hold two light balloons a few centimeters apart and blow fast air straight through the narrow gap between them.
Instead of blowing apart like you expect, the two balloons slam together like invisible magnets.
Fast-moving air between them lost pressure, so calm outside air shoved both spheres straight inward.
Now picture a curved airplane wing slicing horizontally through the sky at hundreds of kilometers an hour.
Air rushing over the curved upper hump speeds up faster than the air gliding smoothly underneath.
Faster air above means lower pressure pushing down, while slower higher-pressure air below shoves the wing skyward.
That tiny pressure difference multiplied across giant wings lifts hundreds of tons of metal right off the runway.
Even a spinning soccer ball uses that exact trick to bend impossibly around a wall of defenders into the goal.
On one side, spin drags air faster and drops pressure; on the opposite side, slower air pushes the ball sideways.
Race cars flip airplane wings completely upside down so fast air sucks their tires tight onto the asphalt track.
Next time you ride in a jet window seat, remember there is no magic holding that cabin in the clouds.
Just millions of air molecules racing faster over the wingtop than underneath, lifting you smoothly across continents.
So the faster the stream moves past your surface, the lighter it pushes—and that simple swap lets steel fly.

## Facts
- Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 Hydrodynamica principle relating higher flow speed to lower static pressure (Wikipedia).
- Aerodynamic lift combines Bernoulli pressure differences and downward flow deflection across cambered airfoils (Wikipedia).
