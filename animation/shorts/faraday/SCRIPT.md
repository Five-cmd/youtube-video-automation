# "The Man Who Climbed Into a Metal Cage to Get Struck by Lightning" — original script v1 (2026-11-09)
Topic: **Electromagnetism & Electrostatic Shielding** (major physics topic). Format: cartoon-3d-short skill (~62 s, ~3.7 words/s), one steady voice, English only, no captions.

## Facts (each joke is a real fact)
- In January 1836 at the Royal Institution in London, Michael Faraday built a 12-foot (3.7 m) cube-shaped wooden frame covered in copper wire and tinfoil, insulated from the floor on glass supports. (Wikipedia, "Faraday cage"; Royal Institution archives)
- He stepped inside with sensitive gold-leaf electrometers while his lecture room generator threw high-voltage sparks onto the outside of the cube. (same)
- Inside the conducting shell, his instruments showed zero electric field even while sparks crackled off the outer corners. (same)
- Like charges repel according to Coulomb's / Gauss's law, distributing exclusively on the outer surface of a conductor so their interior fields cancel. (standard electromagnetism)

## Script
He locked himself inside a metal cage and ordered his assistant: "Shoot me with lightning!"
It's 1836 in London, and Michael Faraday is standing inside a giant box wrapped in copper wire and tinfoil in a packed lecture hall.
Outside the box sits a hand-cranked lightning machine taller than a man.
His assistant spins the glass wheel, sparks hiss into the air, and the front row ducks behind their hats.
Inside the metal box, Faraday calmly holds a delicate gold-leaf detector right against the wall.
Then a blinding blue bolt slams straight into the tinfoil right beside his fingers, and the gold leaf doesn't even twitch.
Luckily, electricity hates its own company.
Unluckily, the people sitting in the front row were not inside the box.
You see, identical electric charges push each other as far apart as they can possibly get.
Ever been stuck in a small room where everybody hates everybody else?
Every single person backs away until they are pressed flat against the outer walls, leaving the middle of the room completely empty.
When lightning hits a metal shell, the charges shove each other to the outside skin, and their pulls cancel out everywhere inside.
That afternoon gave physics the Faraday cage, the shield that protects everything from hospital scanners to astronauts.
Which is why an airliner can take a direct bolt of lightning while you sit inside complaining about the peanuts.
