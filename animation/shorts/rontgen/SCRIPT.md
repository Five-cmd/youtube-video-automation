# "The Invisible Ray That Let a Scientist See His Own Wife's Bones"
Topic: **Discovery of X-Rays** (Wilhelm Röntgen, 1895). Format: punchy documentary short (~64 s).

## Facts
- On November 8, 1895, Wilhelm Röntgen noticed a coated screen glowing across his dark lab even though his cathode-ray tube was wrapped in black cardboard.
- He discovered an invisible penetrating ray ("X" for unknown) that passed through flesh but stopped at bone and metal.
- The first human X-ray photograph was his wife Anna Bertha's hand showing her wedding ring and bones, making her exclaim "I have seen my death!"

## Script
In 1895, a German physicist looked at a glowing green screen in a pitch-black room and accidentally invented medical vision.
Wilhelm Röntgen was testing an electrical glass tube wrapped tightly inside thick black cardboard so zero light could escape.
Nobody expected anything special—until a paper screen nine feet away suddenly started glowing in the dark.
Röntgen rubbed his eyes, turned the tube off, and the mysterious green glow vanished instantly.
He turned it back on, held his own hand in front of the wrapped tube, and froze in pure shock.
Right on the glowing screen, he could see the dark shadow of his living finger bones floating inside his skin.
He called his wife Bertha into the dark lab and asked her to rest her hand over a photographic plate for fifteen minutes.
When the image developed, it showed every bone in her fingers plus her giant wedding ring—and she gasped, "I have seen my death!"
Luckily, Röntgen refused to patent his discovery, giving it free to every hospital on Earth.
Unluckily, nobody knew radiation was dangerous yet, so shoe stores actually used X-rays just to check shoe sizes.
You see, X-rays carry way more energy than ordinary light, so they zip straight through soft skin like ghosts through walls.
Imagine throwing tiny marbles at a chain-link fence—they fly through the gaps, but bounce right off solid steel posts.
Soft flesh lets X-rays pass through, while dense calcium bones block them and cast a crisp shadow.
Within weeks, doctors were finding bullets, broken bones, and swallowed coins without ever picking up a scalpel.
Every airport scanner and hospital X-ray today exists because one scientist noticed a weird glow in the dark.
