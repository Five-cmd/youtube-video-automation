# "Why the Moon Doesn't Crash Straight Into Your Backyard"
Topic: **Universal Gravitation & Orbital Motion** (Isaac Newton, 1666). Format: punchy documentary short (~65 s).

## Facts
- During the 1665–1666 plague lockdown in Woolsthorpe, Isaac Newton saw an apple fall and wondered if the same gravity reached the Moon.
- Newton realized an orbit is perpetual free fall: an object moving fast enough sideways misses the curving Earth as it falls.
- Orbital speed balances inward gravitational pull with sideways inertia.

## Script
Why does an apple fall straight down, while the giant Moon above us never crashes into the Earth?
In 1666, a plague shut down Cambridge University, and a twenty-three-year-old Isaac Newton was stuck in his mother's orchard.
While townsfolk panicked about the lock-down, Newton sat under a tree staring up at the daytime Moon.
Suddenly, a ripe apple snapped off a branch and bonked right beside his boots.
Everyone else would have just eaten the apple and moved on with their day.
But Newton asked a question nobody had ever connected before: if gravity pulls the apple, why doesn't it pull the Moon down too?
He sketched a cannon on top of a massive mountain, firing faster and faster across the sky.
At normal speed, the cannonball drops—but fire it fast enough, and the Earth curves away beneath it forever.
Luckily, the Moon is moving sideways at over two thousand miles an hour.
Unluckily, if it ever stopped moving sideways, we'd have about four days before total impact.
You see, the Moon actually IS falling toward Earth every single second of its life.
Imagine swinging a bucket of water in a circle over your head—the rope pulls inward, while your swing keeps it flying outward.
Gravity is that invisible rope holding the Moon, and sideways speed keeps it missing our planet.
That one orchard afternoon united falling apples and orbiting worlds under a single mathematical law.
Every satellite giving you GPS right now is just playing Newton's falling-and-missing game above your head.
