# Why Hot Iron Glows Red, White, Then Blue

## Script
Step inside a blacksmith's forge and watch what happens when a bar of dark iron heats up inside glowing coals.
At first, it looks completely black to your eyes, even though your hand can already feel fierce invisible heat radiating off it.
Pump the bellows harder, and the iron blushes a deep cherry red as its temperature climbs past five hundred degrees.
Keep stoking the fire, and that cherry glow brightens into fiery orange, then sunny yellow, then blinding white-hot brilliance.
In the year 1900, German physicists hit a bizarre wall trying to explain why hot objects shift color in that exact order.
Old physics math predicted any glowing oven should instantly blast infinite deadly ultraviolet light—a crisis called the ultraviolet catastrophe.
Real ovens obviously never exploded with infinite UV rays, leaving top scientists completely stumped by a piece of hot metal.
Then a quiet professor named Max Planck tried a desperate math trick: what if light energy only leaves in tiny packets?
Instead of pouring out smoothly like water from a hose, glowing heat had to jump out in discrete chunks called quanta.
Suddenly, his weird energy-packet equation matched every forge, campfire, and glowing filament across the entire world.
As temperature rises, atoms jiggle faster, packing enough energy to launch shorter, bluer light packets instead of lazy red ones.
That same color thermometer lets astronomers measure the heat of distant stars without ever leaving Earth.
Cool red giant stars simmer around three thousand degrees, while blazing blue stars burn past twenty thousand degrees.
Even your own body glows right now in invisible infrared light, shining brightly through night-vision cameras.
So the next time a stove coil turns bright orange, remember it took Max Planck cracking open quantum physics to explain why.
