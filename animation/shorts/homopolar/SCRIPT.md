# The Spinning Wire That Built Every Electric Motor

## Script
In 1821, a twenty-nine-year-old lab assistant named Michael Faraday filled a shallow glass bowl with silvery liquid mercury.
He stood a solid bar magnet upright in the center of the dish and dangled a stiff copper wire from above.
Nobody on Earth had ever turned steady electric battery current into continuous spinning motion before.
Faraday clipped the battery wires onto the copper hook and stepped back to watch the hanging tip.
The instant current flowed through the wire and into the conductive mercury pool, something eerie happened.
Without any hand touching it, the copper wire swung outward and began whipping round and round the magnet in circles.
Faster and faster it orbited like an invisible wind was pushing it endlessly around the iron pole.
Faraday danced around his workbench shouting in pure joy—he had just built the world's very first electric motor.
The secret was a hidden tug-of-war: electric current creates circular magnetic rings wrapping tightly around the wire.
Those magnetic rings push sideways against the standing bar magnet, shoving the wire forward around the loop forever.
Because liquid mercury let the spinning tip glide smoothly while staying electrically connected, the rotation never stopped.
Imagine paddling a canoe where the water itself keeps pushing your paddle sideways around a buoy in endless laps.
Faraday replaced the noisy steam boiler with a quiet copper wire and a magnet smaller than a coffee mug.
That single spinning dish scaled up into ceiling fans, blenders, bullet trains, and electric cars humming silently down highways.
Every motor spinning in your home right now traces back to one dancing wire inside a bowl of mercury.
