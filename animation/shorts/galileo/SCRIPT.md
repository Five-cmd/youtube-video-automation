# "He Dropped Two Balls Off a Tower to Humiliate Every Professor in Europe"
Topic: **Free Fall & Gravity** (Galileo Galilei, 1589). Format: punchy documentary short (~64 s).

## Facts
- For nearly 2,000 years, Aristotle's physics claimed heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones in proportion to their weight.
- According to Vincenzo Viviani's biography, Galileo Galilei dropped two spheres of different masses from the Leaning Tower of Pisa in 1589 to show they hit the ground together.
- Air resistance slows flat/light objects like feathers, but in a vacuum (or dense spheres) gravity accelerates all masses equally (~9.8 m/s²).

## Script
For two thousand years, every genius on Earth believed a lie about falling objects.
In 1589, a twenty-five-year-old rebel named Galileo decided to prove them all wrong in public.
He climbed to the top of the Leaning Tower of Pisa carrying two solid iron cannonballs.
One was ten times heavier than the other, and a crowd of angry professors gathered below to watch him fail.
Aristotle had written that the heavy ball must slam down ten times faster.
Questioning Aristotle in the sixteenth century wasn't just rude—it could cost you your career.
Galileo leaned over the stone railing, held both spheres side by side, and counted down.
Then he let go of both balls at the exact same instant—and they hit the cobblestone together with a single boom.
Luckily, Galileo proved gravity pulls every mass equally.
Unluckily, the humiliated professors still ran him out of town.
You see, gravity gives every single falling object the exact same acceleration, no matter how heavy it is.
Drop a bowling ball and a marble from the same height, and they race neck and neck all the way down.
The only reason a feather loses that race is air pushing up against its wide surface.
Take the air away on the Moon, and a hammer and a feather hit the dust together.
So the next time someone says heavier things fall faster, remind them that Galileo settled that argument four hundred years ago.
